Every phishing guide gives you a list of things to check. Almost nobody runs a list while standing in a queue with a notification on screen. So this one is shorter, and built around the two or three signals that survive being busy.

## The only three signals worth memorising

**It came to you.** Legitimate institutions rarely initiate a request that requires you to act inside their message. If the message brought you the link, treat the link as untrusted.

**It wants speed.** Blocked accounts, expiring refunds, penalties by midnight. Real deadlines exist, but they do not arrive by SMS with a shortened link.

**It asks you to approve, install or share.** Approving a payment request, installing a support app, reading out a code. Any of the three, from an unexpected message, is the end of the conversation.

## What the good ones look like

Assume perfect spelling. Assume the right logo and the right colours. Assume the sender name reads correctly on your phone, because display names are trivially spoofed and your phone hides the actual address.

Sophisticated attempts also reference something true — a recent order, a real bank, a service you actually use. That detail is often scraped or guessed, and it is there precisely to disarm the check you were about to make.

## If you already tapped

Speed matters more than embarrassment.

1. **Disconnect and change the password** for the account involved, from a different device if you can.
2. **Tell the bank or platform immediately.** Reporting time affects both recovery and, later, any claim.
3. **Check your email account** for forwarding rules or new recovery addresses. This is the step almost everyone skips, and it is how attackers keep access after a password change.
4. **Write down the timeline** while it is fresh: what arrived, when, what you did, what you saw.

## Where insurance fits

Reporting promptly and preserving evidence is what makes the rest possible. Depending on the policy, cover may respond to eligible losses arising from a covered phishing or spoofing incident, and to certain costs of establishing how the compromise occurred.
