Most people picture payment fraud as someone breaking into a bank. It is almost never that. In the overwhelming majority of cases, the money leaves because a real person was persuaded to let it, or because a credential was reused somewhere it should not have been.

## The sequence

It usually runs in four steps.

**1. Contact.** A message, a call, or a listing. It arrives in a context that makes sense — a refund you were expecting, a delivery you are waiting for, a KYC deadline you half-remember reading about.

**2. Pressure.** A reason to act now. An account about to be blocked, a refund about to expire, a buyer who needs to pay immediately. Urgency is the active ingredient; it removes the pause where you would normally check.

**3. Redirection.** You are moved somewhere the attacker controls: a link, a payment request, a "collect" instead of a "send", or an app installed for support.

**4. Authorisation.** You approve it. This is the part people find hardest to accept afterwards — the transaction was technically valid, because you completed it.

## Why "just be careful" is not enough advice

The messages are good. They use real logos, real formatting and real branch names. Some arrive from numbers that have genuinely been used by the institution before. The assumption that victims were careless is both wrong and unhelpful — the people targeted most successfully are busy, not naive.

## Three habits that break the sequence

- **Never approve a request you did not initiate.** In UPI, money is *sent*, not *collected*. A request to approve something in order to receive money is the single most common pattern.
- **Verify on a number you already had.** Not the number in the message. Not a number you searched for. One you already held.
- **Turn on two-factor authentication for your email first.** Email is the reset path for everything else. Securing it protects accounts you have forgotten you own.

## Where insurance fits

None of the above is insurance. It is what reduces the chance of ever needing it, and we would rather you did it.

What insurance addresses is the part good habits cannot: the loss that happens anyway. Depending on the policy, cover may respond to eligible financial loss from unauthorised transactions, and to the costs of investigating and documenting what happened. Cover generally depends on reporting promptly, and every policy carries limits, deductibles and exclusions.
